# Before-and-after editorial examples

These fictional excerpts illustrate revisions for a general reader learning about marketing funnels. They can be adapted into review explanations. All counts are illustrative, and the examples do not report a product experiment. Choose examples suited to the audience rather than copying this story into every guide.

The shared example is a meal-planning app: a parent views a sample week, starts a trial, saves their own weekly menu with a grocery list, subscribes, returns to plan another week, and recommends the app to a friend.

## Establish the purpose before the measurement

**Before**

> Twelve of the 18 buyers return during days 30–44 after their first payment. The repeat-use rate is 66.7%.

**After**

> A subscription tells us someone chose to pay. We also want to know whether the app keeps helping them plan dinners. Following buyers into another week helps investigate that continued usefulness. In this example, 12 of the 18 buyers save another menu and grocery list during days 30–44 after payment. All 18 have completed the window, so each has had the same opportunity to qualify. The repeat-use rate is about 66.7%.

**Editorial decision:** Establish what repeat use helps us learn before presenting its rate. Retain the observation window and eligibility condition needed to interpret the count.

## Choose a story with little unrelated background

**Before**

> Consider an app for freelancers whose CSV files contain dates that an importer rejects.

**After**

> Consider a meal-planning app for busy parents who want to spend less time deciding what to cook. It helps them choose dinners for the week and combines the ingredients into a grocery list.

**Editorial decision:** The first story leaves a general reader needing to understand files, import software, and rejected date formats before understanding the customer's problem. The second makes the need and useful result familiar. The technical story could still suit a guide for readers who work with data imports.

## Interpret the subject shown by the visual

**Before**

> Read the bands from left to right. Their widths represent people. A gray band shows people who did not complete the next action.

**After**

> Of the 60 trial users, 36 save a weekly menu and grocery list within 14 days of their first visit; the other 24 do not. These 24 people started trying the app but did not finish a plan they could shop from. Their experience gives us a specific part of the trial to investigate.

**Editorial decision:** Interpret what the funnel reveals about customers. Keep necessary units and windows in the figure or nearby text; explain chart conventions when they affect understanding. The count identifies a question, without establishing why people stopped.

## Let a finding motivate the next section

**Before**

> The overview connects all six stages.
>
> Before using a rate to choose work, write down the measurement rules.

**After**

> The 24 trial users without a saved menu give us a question to investigate: what prevented them from finishing their plan? First, check whether the count matches their experience. A missing record could mean the menu was never saved, or that it was saved without the action being recorded. Those explanations lead to different work.

**Editorial decision:** The unfinished plans provide a reason to check the records. The transition develops the investigation instead of changing abruptly from an overview to a checklist.

## Make a synthesis add understanding

**Before**

> The journey includes awareness, interest, consideration, conversion, retention, and referral. Each stage has a count.

**After**

> Of the 600 visitors, 420 do not open the sample menu. It is the largest loss, but those visitors might want a single recipe rather than a weekly plan, or might not see how to begin. Further along, 24 trial users do not save their own menu. Because they have started a trial, the investigation can focus more closely on choosing meals and finishing a plan. The largest loss and the most specific question need not occur at the same stage.

**Editorial decision:** Compare the parts to explain what they suggest about choosing an investigation. The possible causes remain hypotheses; a smaller group does not automatically deserve priority.
